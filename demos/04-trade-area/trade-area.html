The panel is not the population

A mobile footfall panel is a sample, and a badly biased one: penetration runs nearly six times higher in young urban segments than in older rural ones. Expanding it with a single national factor — which is what almost everyone does first — inflates the market by a fifth, because the panel over-represents exactly the people who use this format most.

Calibrating the gravity model

The distance decay is not assumed, it is estimated — by maximum likelihood on the choice shares the panel actually observed. Because panel penetration is a single factor per output area, it cancels out of the within-area shares entirely: the decay parameter comes from the raw counts, and only the volumes need expanding.

Does it explain how sites actually trade?

A catchment model cannot see manager quality, frontage, seating or whether there is a bus stop outside. Explaining four fifths of the variance in site revenue from geography and demographics alone is the evidence that the forecast for a site that does not exist yet is worth something.

The forty-site plan

Three numbers, and the distance between them is the finding. The plan values new sites at estate-average revenue. The model says the marginal sites are worth a fraction of that — and a third of what they do generate comes off stores the buyer already owns.

      About this data

      Tarnbrook Bakehouse is synthetic. The generator builds a region of output areas with population and Mosaic mix, places the estate and a competing field, then runs a gravity model with a known distance decay to produce true visits and true site revenue. It then samples a mobile panel from those visits with deliberate demographic bias in penetration.
