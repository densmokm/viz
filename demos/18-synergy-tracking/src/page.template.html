From the deal model to what arrives

Four things sit between the number in the model and the number that reaches EBITDA: owners re-estimating their initiatives, initiatives that never convert, initiatives that convert for less than they promised, and dis-synergies the model never carried at all.

Cost is landing. Revenue is not.

This is the pattern in nearly every integration, and the reason a single blended completion percentage is worse than useless: it averages a programme that is working with one that is not, and the blend looks survivable until it is too late to act.

Delivered and banked initiatives are excluded — they are already counted at realised value. Revenue converts materially worse from every open stage, and a PMO confidence rating that ignores this is the least reliable number in a synergy tracker.

Where the programme actually lands

Twenty thousand simulations over the register: each initiative converts or does not, at the rate its stage and type earn from history, and lands at a realisation factor drawn from the same history. The result is not a range of opinions — it is what this pipeline can produce.

Run-rate is not EBITDA

An initiative delivered in month twelve carries its full annualised value into the run-rate and almost none of it into this year's P&L. Reporting one as the other is how a programme looks fine right up until the audited numbers arrive.

    Method notes

      About this data

      Wexford and Pentland are synthetic. The generator models initiatives across eight workstreams with realistic stage distributions, cost-to-achieve overruns where the work is hard, and a library of initiatives from prior deals with known outcomes. Every probability in the forecast is estimated from that library; none is set by hand.

      The bridge reconciles to . A synergy tracker whose numbers do not add up is worse than no tracker at all, because it gets presented to a board.
